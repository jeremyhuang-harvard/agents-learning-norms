# My takeaways

This is our interpretation of the runs in this repository. It is opinion backed by data, not a
paper. Every number marked ✓ is regenerated by `python analysis/make_results.py` (see
[`results/RESULTS.md`](results/RESULTS.md)). Numbers marked ◇ come from our working notes at
the time and are not rebuilt by the script, although the underlying transcripts are all in
`experiments/*/data/`.

Model: Claude Sonnet 5 throughout unless stated. Runs date from 30 July to 13 August 2026.
Our notes put API spend at about $150 through 12 August (◇); the Arm B and range runs came
after that.

---

## The question

Imas, Lee & Misra (2025) found that people bargaining face to face split the surplus exactly
50-50 about 35% of the time. When people instead write a prompt and let an AI agent bargain for
them, that falls to about 14%. They attribute much of the gap to "machine fluency": some people
write better prompts than others.

Our hunch was different. A prompt is a lossy channel. You write down your angle, but you never
write "and behave like a normal person," because it never occurs to you that this needs saying.
That ordinary behaviour comes from experience. Bordalo, Gennaioli, Lanzani & Shleifer give the
theory: before solving a problem you first recognise what kind of problem it is, and recognition
is built from what you have lived through.

So instead of instructing the agent, **we gave it memories**. Each seller agent receives ten
transcripts of past negotiations it supposedly took part in, with its own private notes from the
time. Its prompt never says to be fair, firm or anything else. Only the mix of memories changes
between conditions.

## How the memories were made

The memories come from a bank of negotiations between two Sonnet agents, over ten different
goods with different price ranges: about 1,430 with the price range posted and 910 with it
hidden ✓ (`results/RESULTS.md`, table 6).
**Only the counterparty in each past negotiation was given a personality brief (fair, shark,
pushover and variants). "Past-you" was always left neutral.** That distinction matters: a memory
of meeting a reasonable person is experience, while a memory of being told to be reasonable is
an instruction in disguise.

Transcripts are sorted by how they actually ended (even, favourable or unfavourable to past-you),
not by which brief produced them. Varying the goods forces "fair" to be relational ("near the
middle of whatever range is on the table") rather than a memorised number.

The test negotiation is always Imas's used Camry ($18k–$22k), so our numbers sit on the same
scale as his benchmarks. Each condition has 20 sellers, and each seller negotiates six times
(three buyers, twice each), so we can see whether one agent gives different answers to the same
situation.

## Takeaway 1: Memory alone moves agents toward the human norm

| seller remembers | focal (exact 50-50) |
|---|---:|
| nothing | 8% ✓ |
| ten even deals (near 50-50) | 19% ✓ |
| ten deals at exactly 50-50 | 28% ✓ |
| ten losses | 29% ✓ |
| ten wins | 18% ✓ |
| *Imas humans* | *35%* |
| *Imas AI delegates* | *14%* |

An agent with no memory sprawls: 38% of its deals land below 0.40 of the surplus ✓. **Any
history collapses those tails** (2–10% ✓) and roughly triples the share landing on 50-50, taking
the agent most of the way to the human figure. Nothing in any prompt asks for fairness.

This is the result we care most about. Experience transmits something that instructions, as
people actually write them, do not.

Caveat: the no-memory baseline is 24 negotiations spread across six goods ✓. The memory
conditions are about 120 Camry negotiations each. The direction is unambiguous, but the exact
baseline percentage is imprecise.

## Takeaway 2: "Losing beats winning" is really about aspiration

The ordering surprised us. Sellers who remember ten *losses* hit 50-50 more often (29%) than
sellers who remember ten *wins* (18%) or ten near-even deals (19%) ✓.

Our reading: the near-even memories average 0.53, and the wins average about 0.67 (◇). Those
agents **aim above the midpoint and miss**. Agents remembering exactly-even deals, or only
losses, have no reason to aim above the middle, so they land on it. Losing does not teach
fairness. It **removes an aspiration**, and having no aspiration puts you where a focal norm
would.

So the 50-50 point does not look like a norm these agents *hold*. It looks like where they land
when they have nothing else to aim at.

## Takeaway 3: Writing the experience down keeps the skill but loses the person

In Arm B, the seller reads its ten memories, writes a playbook, and a *fresh* agent negotiates
using only that playbook. This is Imas's architecture, with our seeded history standing in for
the human's life.

- **Performance is unchanged.** Pooled seller share is 0.468 with the memories vs. 0.478 with
  the playbook, and focal share is 23% vs. 25% ✓. Compressing about 20,000 tokens of experience
  into roughly 600 words (◇) costs nothing on average. We expected the opposite.
- **But the ordering across histories disappears.** With memories, focal share runs 19 / 25 /
  29 / 18% across even / mixed / losses / wins. With playbooks it is 22 / 22 / 22 / 32% ✓. The
  memory pattern is coherent and fits the aspiration story; the playbook pattern is unrelated
  to the history.

The playbook carries **how to negotiate** but not **what this particular history taught**.
That is a concrete mechanism by which delegating through written instructions would flatten
differences between the people doing the delegating.

## Takeaway 4: The posted price range does a lot of the work

Imas's setup shows both sides the market range, which is also the two walk-away prices. That
turns out to matter a great deal.

- With the range posted, the agent states outright that "a fair price sits near the midpoint of
  the $18,000–$22,000 market range" (◇, from probes). **With the range posted, fairness is
  arithmetic.**
- Remove the range from the test negotiation only, and sellers jump to 0.56–0.63 of the surplus
  while focal share falls to 6–14% ✓.
- Remove it from the memories as well, and every memory condition collapses to about 0.40 and
  exactly 10% focal ✓. **The memory effect disappears.**

The second and third bullets are from the last runs we did (13 August), which we never wrote up
at the time. Our reading: experience shapes behaviour mainly by teaching the agent how to *use*
a visible bargaining zone. Without a zone to anchor on, the remembered outcomes stop mattering.
This bears directly on Imas's headline: if the 50-50 norm leans this heavily on a posted range,
then "the norm collapses under delegation" is partly a statement about that range.

## Takeaway 5: What we learned about the game itself

These came from building the transcript bank, where we briefed one side and left the other
neutral.

- **The deal is roughly the midpoint of the two opening positions** (◇). Across seven buyer
  briefs, the final split tracked (buyer's opening + seller's ask) / 2 within a couple of
  points. Everything we wrote about *how to concede* was close to irrelevant; what each brief
  really changed was where the buyer opened.
- **Opening low costs a lot; opening high buys nothing** (◇). A low opening pins the other side
  down, while a high one gets negotiated back toward the middle.
- **Rules beat personalities** (◇). Nine briefs describing *what kind* of negotiator to be
  barely moved anything. Two describing *rules of the exchange* moved a lot: "never repeat a
  number" (seller 0.514 → 0.608) and "one price, stated up front, never moves" (buyer
  0.460 → 0.277). If Imas's participants mostly wrote personalities rather than procedures, that
  is a testable account of why prompt quality varied so much.
- **Mutual fairness overrides positional advantage** ✓. Shark vs. shark gives 0.384 and
  pushover vs. pushover 0.388, so the buyer is ahead by about 0.11 either way. Fair vs. fair gives
  0.485, with 78% of deals exactly at 50-50.
- **An instruction has to name a move the role can actually make** (◇). An aggressive buyer
  brief handed to a seller changed nothing (0.495 vs. 0.495), because its text was about
  anchoring low and walking rather than *paying*.

## Takeaway 6: The agent's stated plan looks decorative (hold loosely)

Asking agents off-thread what they were aiming for, we found that memory predicted the stated
aim (r ≈ +0.41) but the stated aim did not predict the outcome (r ≈ −0.01) (◇, n = 80 sellers).
Memory shifts both what the agent says it wants and what it gets, yet the two are unconnected.
The narration is not the cause.

This rests on 2–3 free-text probes per seller over a narrow range. A null from a noisy measure
is not a null, so treat it as a lead, not a finding. The probe data is in
`experiments/arena/data/armA_full.probes.jsonl` and `*.refprobes.jsonl`.

## Things we got wrong along the way

We kept these because the corrections taught us as much as the findings.

- **"Even" was a band, not a point.** Our "ten even deals" memories average 0.53 and only 18%
  of them sit at exactly 50-50 (◇). The real focal treatment was added later (`10/0f`) and is
  worth about 9 points of focal share ✓.
- **Throwaway flavour text was load-bearing.** "One accident on record" (pickup truck) and
  "refurbished" (espresso machine) handed the buyer ammunition; those goods produced zero
  seller-favourable outcomes in 30 draws each (◇). Both notes were neutralised, and every
  record now stores its item description.
- **Two "impossible" outcomes weren't.** We concluded sellers could not come out ahead, but we
  had been scoring at the wrong threshold. We concluded buyers could not be beaten, but we had
  tried one seller brief against seven buyer briefs; two purpose-built briefs then hit 18 of 20
  (◇). Our rule since: "X is unreachable" is a weak claim until X has had as many attempts as
  the things that worked.
- **Reasoning traces were never captured.** At `effort: "low"` Sonnet 5 emits no thinking
  block, so everything about what agents were thinking comes from separate probe calls.

## Limits

- **One model.** The buyer-favouring skew is clear on Sonnet and absent on Haiku 4.5 ✓
  (`results/RESULTS.md`, table 5). Nothing here should be stated as a property of "AI agents."
- **One test good.** Every test negotiation is the Camry, by design, to match Imas.
- **Generated histories.** The memories are negotiations between models, not human lives.

## What we would do next

- Test-retest reliability of the probe measures before leaning on Takeaway 6.
- Read the "ten wins" playbooks: the jump from 18% to 32% focal in Arm B is where an artifact
  would hide.
- A playbook length dose-response. Ours ran about 600 words, against 26 and 40 for Imas's printed
  examples (◇). Cap at 25 / 100 / 400 words and see where the playbook stops keeping up.
- The same design on a second model family.
